Everything below comes from peer-reviewed studies, meta-analyses and systematic reviews. Confidence tags: strong several RCTs or a meta-analysis agree · moderate one good RCT or consistent small studies · weak single small study or indirect · null good evidence of no effect. Full reference list at the bottom.

The short version

  • For plain reading, silence is at least as good as any sound. A Bayesian meta-analysis of 65 studies found noise, music and speech all slightly impair reading; speech and lyrics are worst. strong [23]
  • Sound earns its place by masking. Working memory is disrupted by changing sound (talking, music with hooks, intermittent noise), not by steady sound. A steady noise bed hides those changes. strong [33]
  • Rapid amplitude modulation (12–16 Hz) on a noise/music bed is the best-supported "focus sound". See the entrainment section. moderate
  • Binaural beats: small, inconsistent effects. Beta (≈16 Hz) and gamma (40 Hz) are the only bands with positive attention findings; theta/delta beats worsen vigilance. Treat them as optional. weak
  • Brown noise has no specific evidence. Nothing separates it from pink or white. Pick whichever you find least annoying. null [4, 11]
  • Nature sound before a block lowers stress and restores attention. strong for stress [30], moderate for attention [29]
  • Retrieval practice and spacing beat everything else you can do while studying. Sound is a minor variable next to these. strong [40–44]
  • Keep it quiet. Benefits for neurotypical adults appear at ≈45 dB; 65 dB raised stress. WHO safe-listening budget is 80 dB for 40 h/week. [9, 47]

Why the protocols look the way they do

PhaseWhy
Prime (3 min)Nature soundscape lowers heart rate and self-reported stress (meta-analysis, g = −0.60) and modestly restores directed attention when played before a task. Use it to set the block's goal and write 2–3 questions to answer at the end. [29, 30]
Focus (25–50 min)Block length does not change how much you learn (two RCTs, N = 87 and N = 176), but fixed blocks prevent over-long fatiguing sessions. Vigilance starts to sag after 20–40 min. The 90-minute "ultradian cycle" has no physiological support (N = 60, sampled every 10 min for 9 h: no rhythm). moderate [34, 35, 37, 39]
Recall (book closed)Practice testing is the single best-supported technique: g ≈ 0.5–0.6 versus re-reading across 150+ effect sizes. Close the notes and retrieve from memory: blank-page recall, flashcards, answer the prime questions. strong [40–42]
Break (5–10 min)Micro-breaks restore vigour and reduce fatigue (d ≈ 0.35, N = 2,335) but only lift performance after heavy tasks if they run longer than 10 min. Spend the first 2–3 minutes eyes closed, no phone: wakeful rest after learning boosts later recall (pooled d = 0.38), and it needs an unoccupied mind. strong for well-being, moderate for memory [36, 45, 46]
Spaced re-testRe-quiz the material at +1 day, +1 week, then at roughly 10–20 % of the time left until the exam (N = 1,350). Not something this app times for you; use flashcards. strong [43, 44]
Wind-down / sleepThe famous pink-noise memory results used EEG-timed clicks during deep sleep, which a phone cannot do, and even those shrink on replication (d ≈ 0.15 pooled). Continuous pink noise all night gave no memory gain and possibly hurt insight (N = 72). The Monroe preset is offered for relaxation only. null for memory [14–19, 21]

Background sound: what the studies found

  • White/pink noise and attention. Meta-analysis (2024, 24 studies): small benefit in ADHD (g = +0.25) and small harm in non-ADHD youth (g = −0.21). Benefit is confined to the inattentive profile; a third of ADHD children did worse. Lab levels were 70–80 dB, which is loud. strong [1–5]
  • Level matters for everyone else. In a private-office RCT, 45 dB white noise improved sustained attention, accuracy and creativity and lowered stress; 65 dB helped working memory but raised stress. moderate [9]
  • New-word learning. One RCT (N = 80) found 70 dB white noise during learning improved recall trajectory in healthy adults. Single lab, small effect. moderate [7]
  • Working memory maintenance. Noise during the hold period of a working-memory task hurt accuracy (N = 167). So: noise while reading, not while doing mental arithmetic. moderate [8]
  • Coffee-shop noise. 70 dB café ambience helped idea generation, 50 and 85 dB did not. This is a creativity result, not a focus result; reading gets worse. moderate for creativity only [12]
  • Music. Lyrics impair reading and memory whether you like the song or not (N = 30). Instrumental lo-fi was neutral: no harm, no benefit. The Mozart effect does not exist (meta-analysis, 3,000+ participants). Fast and loud disrupts; soft and slow does not. strong [22, 24, 25, 27, 28]
  • Rain. One study found rain helped hard arithmetic, but at 89 dB, which is unsafe. Treat rain as a pleasant nature mask, nothing more. weak [32]
  • Open-plan noise. 51 dBA office noise versus 39 dBA cut word recall and raised fatigue over 2 h; a river soundscape during the break improved energy. moderate [13]

Hearing safety

WHO: below 80 dB is unlikely to cause damage; 80 dB for 40 h/week is the budget, and it halves for every +3 dB. Keep the phone under 60 % of max volume for long sessions. A 6-hour study day at 65–70 dB is fine; the 75–80 dB levels used in the ADHD studies burn the weekly budget fast. [47, 48]

Brainwave entrainment: binaural, isochronic, and modulated noise

This is the part most apps oversell. Two meta-analyses pool a medium effect for binaural beats (g ≈ 0.40–0.45), but the underlying studies are small, and the best-powered direct tests of the most-cited claims are null. EEG entrainment by binaural beats is inconsistent (5 of 14 EEG studies supportive). What survives:

  • Rapid amplitude modulation at ≈16 Hz on a noise or music bed improved sustained attention versus unmodulated sound in four experiments (N = 83, 34, 40, 175), with the strongest benefit in people with high ADHD-trait scores, and a novelty effect on first exposure. The authors work for a company selling it, and no independent replication exists yet. This is the app's default focus sound. moderate [70, 71]
  • Beta binaural beats (≈16 Hz) for vigilance: the classic 1998 result (N = 29, no silent control) did not hold up in a three-experiment replication that found no consistent effect. null [53, 54]
  • Beta binaural beats (15–20 Hz) for memory encoding: 20 Hz improved long-term recall and 15 Hz improved working-memory accuracy by about 3 %, while 5 Hz theta impaired both. Small single-site studies. This is why the Memorise protocol uses β16 and why no preset uses theta during study. weak [59, 60, 61]
  • Gamma 40 Hz binaural: mixed. A 2025 parametric study (N = 64) found 40 Hz on a 340 Hz carrier raised overall hits but did not stop the vigilance decline; another (N = 58) found nothing on any attention network. Attentional-focus results from one lab point in opposite directions and one of their papers was retracted. 40 Hz is above the limit where a beat is actually heard (≈30 Hz), but it still drives a measurable 40 Hz brain response. weak [55, 56, 57, 62]
  • 40 Hz and dementia: the mouse amyloid-clearance work and the small Alzheimer's trials use 1 h/day for months, combine light and sound, and depend on amyloid pathology. None of it transfers to a healthy student. null for students [72, 73]
  • Isochronic and monaural beats entrain EEG more strongly than binaural and work on speakers, but no controlled study shows them improving attention or learning in healthy adults. Offered as an option, not a recommendation. null for cognition [52, 68]
  • Individual differences are real. The same 40 Hz stimulus removed the attentional blink only in people with a low spontaneous eye-blink rate (a dopamine proxy) and did nothing or worse for others. Expect some people to get worse; the tone volume slider goes to zero for a reason. weak [63, 64]
  • Anxiety: theta/delta binaural beats reduce state anxiety (pooled g = 0.69 in cognition studies; SMD −1.38 across 15 perioperative RCTs, N = 1,047, mostly unblinded). Useful before an exam, not during study: 30 min of 7 Hz theta lowered mood and immediate recall in one crossover. Alpha 10 Hz is the safer calming choice. moderate for anxiety [49, 61, 74]
  • Sleep onset: 3 Hz binaural on a 250 Hz carrier added about 35 min of deep sleep (N = 23). No memory outcome was measured. weak [75]
  • Timing and carrier: exposure before plus during a task beats during-only, and 10 min of lead-in helped in the 2025 study, which is what the Prime phase is for. Carriers of 250–400 Hz work best; 340 Hz beat 400 Hz in the one head-to-head. Masking noise does not reduce efficacy in the meta-analysis, and in one study the noise, not the beat, carried the behavioural benefit. moderate [49, 51, 55]

Safety

No serious adverse event has been reported for audio-only entrainment across the meta-analyses and trials reviewed (over 1,000 participants in the anxiety RCTs alone). Documented mild effects: drowsiness, transient anxiety, and lowered mood or recall after prolonged theta. Sound-triggered epilepsy exists but is about 1 in 10 million and is triggered by emotionally salient music, not rhythmic pulses; photosensitive epilepsy is a visual risk and does not apply to audio. Pacemaker and pregnancy warnings appear on vendor sites and have no evidential basis. The one real risk is hearing: see the WHO limits above. Do not use delta or theta presets while driving. [47, 73, 74]
